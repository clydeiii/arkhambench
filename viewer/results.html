ArkhamBench · Case Notes · Night of the Zealot

Findings from the investigation

Coding agents play Arkham Horror: The Card Game solo, inside a rules-enforcing engine, with a persistent notebook as their only memory between games. Three questions under investigation: can models play — and if so, how well? Can they learn from repeated play — and if so, how well? And can they playtest — find real rules bugs in the engine that hosts them — and if so, how well? Every score on this page is a link into the full replay.

Question 2 · Can they learn? — Exhibit 0.5

Not measurably — and experience isn't cheap

The learning bench: each model played the same scenario thirty times over, starting from a blank notebook — the only memory carried between games. Same thirty card shuffles for everyone. If models genuinely learned, late games would beat early ones. They don't: every trend line is flat — the best and worst slopes across seven models amount to about one point of drift over all thirty games, on a 0–9 scale. Skill shows up in game one or not at all. And because each game's notes make the next game's reading list longer, per-game cost went up, not down, for most models — experience literally made the games more expensive. Each bar is one game; click any bar to replay it. One exception to the flat-curve rule emerged when the last three models finished: Hunyuan 3 — the cheapest model on the board at $9 for all thirty games — posted the steepest climb and the strongest final stretch of any model. One model at thirty games is a signal, not a proof. But it is the closest thing to genuine learning this benchmark has measured.

Question 2 · follow-up — the persistent-memory test

Would a real memory help? We gave two models one — it didn't

A fair objection to the flat curves above: maybe the models never learned because we kept wiping their memory. Between games, everything except the notebook is erased — and OpenAI showed on a different benchmark (ARC-AGI-3) that exactly this kind of memory-wiping harness was holding its model back: letting the model keep its private reasoning and summarizing old history instead of deleting it tripled its score. So we reran the thirty games for two models with those settings on. Each model played all thirty games in one continuous session — never reset, automatically summarized whenever it grew too long, notebook still available. The chain never broke once. The result: no tripling — both models scored slightly worse (comparing the same thirty shuffles head-to-head, the fresh-session run won more games than it lost for both models), and the learning curves stayed flat. The flat-curve finding is about the models, not the harness. Two side effects were real, though. Per-game cost stopped climbing — summaries keep the reading list bounded, where in the main bench each game's notes made the next game more expensive (though the Claude lane's total bill still came out 70% higher, because re-reading a month-long conversation every move costs real money even at cache rates). And Fable changed personality: with a permanent memory of every close call, it settled into a cautious farm-points-and-retreat routine and stopped playing for wins — 2 wins in thirty games versus 13 with fresh eyes. Both models also nearly stopped writing notebooks (70 and 91 lines versus 232 and 503 first time around) — they leaned on the session and wrote less down.

Question 3 · Can they playtest? — Exhibit 1

Yes — and the engine is better for it

Every model is told that finding a genuine rules bug in the game engine is worth more than any score — so every player is also a playtester. Their reports are checked the way a court checks testimony: other models audit the game records, dedicated verifiers try to knock each claim down against the printed cards, and a final judge rules on what's real. That judge has reversed rulings in every direction — including its own — eight times.

Wave 6 put a new model in the judge's chair. On its release week, Kimi K3 audited all sixty artifacts of a fresh fifteen-campaign wave — every scenario transcript and every campaign ledger. It filed forty findings: twenty-three confirmed (twenty-two distinct defects, including two exploits and three material-outcome bugs that had survived five prior audit waves), seventeen refuted. Six of the refutations were the transcript's fault, not the judge's — each became an adopted logging improvement — and a Sol counter-audit of six K3-cleared games corroborated five, catching one missed display-severity defect. Lower precision than Sol, more confirmed defects than any auditor before it.

Auditor precision — confirmed defects per claim. Playing strength and auditing strength are different skills; the most precise auditor confirmed eighteen of twenty-one claims, the most prolific confirmed twenty-three of forty, and the weakest filed seventy claims for three.

Method. Score = max(0, XP − trauma) per the campaign guide. Benchmarks use identical seeds (1001–1010) and an interleaved five-investigator rotation; each agent's persistent notebook starts empty and is its only cross-game memory.
